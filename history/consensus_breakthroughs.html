Breakthroughs in Consensus#

blockchainkit.consensus hub · History · Examples · API reference

“The longest chain not only serves as proof of the sequence of events witnessed, but proof that it came from the largest pool of CPU power.” – Satoshi Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System, 2008

Signatures say who authorized a payment; they cannot say which of two conflicting payments came first. Consensus mechanisms answer that question, either by voting among known participants or by making some resource scarce: computation, or stake. This chronology traces the ideas behind blockchainkit.consensus along three threads that meet in Bitcoin and Ethereum. Voting among known participants runs from the Byzantine generals to PBFT. Scarce computation runs from pricing via processing to Nakamoto consensus, with the gambler’s ruin supplying the security argument. Scarce stake runs from Peercoin to Casper finality and Algorand’s committees. The models are deliberately idealized; Exact conventions and model boundaries lists where they depart from deployed protocols.

1656 – The Gambler’s Ruin: Catching Up from Behind#

In 1656 Pascal posed to Fermat the problem of two gamblers playing until one is ruined, and Huygens published it the next year. For a walk that steps toward the goal with probability \(q\) and away with \(p = 1 - q\), the chance of ever making up a deficit of \(z\) is

\[\begin{split}P_z = \begin{cases} 1 & q \ge p, \\ (q/p)^{z} & q < p. \end{cases}\end{split}\]

An attacker mining a secret chain is exactly this player. Each new block is one step: it is the attacker’s, closing the gap by one, with probability \(q\) equal to the attacker’s share of the hashrate, and otherwise the honest network’s, widening it by one. Section 11 of the Bitcoin whitepaper applies the result to an attacker racing the honest chain: below half the hashrate, the chance of ever catching up falls exponentially with every block of lead.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.catch_up.eventual_catch_up().

References: C. Huygens, De ratiociniis in ludo aleae (1657), problem 5; the problem was posed in the Pascal-Fermat correspondence of 1656.

The gambler’s ruin: catching up from behind (Pascal, Fermat and Huygens 1656)

1982 – The Byzantine Generals Problem#

Generals surrounding a city must all attack or all retreat, but some are traitors who tell different generals different things. Lamport, Shostak and Pease gave the problem its name and its answer: with oral (unsigned) messages, the loyal generals can agree despite \(m\) traitors if and only if

\[n \ge 3m + 1.\]

Their algorithm OM(\(m\)) has each lieutenant relay the order it received, recursively for \(m\) rounds, and decide by majority. The bound is tight already for three generals and one traitor. If the commander says “attack” to one lieutenant and “retreat” to the other, the first lieutenant hears “attack” from the commander and “retreat” relayed by its peer. A loyal commander and a lying peer would produce exactly the same messages, so the lieutenant cannot tell which one is the traitor, and no rule can be right in both cases. With signed messages a forged relay would be detected, and the bound disappears. Later papers write the number of faults as \(f\), and the \(3f + 1\) bound reappears in every Byzantine fault-tolerant blockchain protocol.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.byzantine.oral_messages() runs OM(\(m\)) with a configurable traitor strategy.

References: L. Lamport, R. Shostak, and M. Pease, The Byzantine Generals Problem, ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems 4(3), 382–401 (1982). DOI.

The Byzantine generals problem (Lamport, Shostak and Pease 1982)

1983 – Ben-Or’s Randomized Consensus#

The same year, Fischer, Lynch and Paterson presented the impossibility result of the 1985 entry below: no deterministic protocol can guarantee agreement in an asynchronous network where a process may crash. Michael Ben-Or escaped it by letting processes flip coins, giving the first asynchronous consensus protocol that tolerates \(f\) crashes among \(n > 2f\) processes.

Each round has two exchanges, and in each a process waits for only \(n - f\) messages, because the other \(f\) may never arrive. First, every process reports its current value. A process then proposes \(v\) if more than \(n/2\) of the reports it received are \(v\), a strict majority of all processes, and otherwise proposes nothing. Two values cannot both have a strict majority, so all proposals in a round name the same value. A process decides \(v\) after receiving \(f + 1\) proposals of \(v\), adopts \(v\) after receiving at least one, and otherwise flips a coin for its next value. Once all correct processes hold the same value, every one of them proposes it and decides. Fresh coins are flipped in every round, so that moment comes sooner or later.

Against any message scheduler, termination therefore has probability one. With \(n\) independent coins the expected number of rounds can be exponential in \(n\), which later common-coin protocols reduced to a constant.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.randomized.ben_or(), with an adversarial or random message scheduler.

References: M. Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols, PODC ‘83, 27–30 (1983). DOI.

Randomized consensus: agreeing by flipping coins (Ben-Or 1983)

Randomized consensus: agreeing by flipping coins (Ben-Or 1983)

1985 – FLP: No Deterministic Consensus in an Asynchronous Network#

Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that if messages can be delayed arbitrarily and even one process might crash, every deterministic protocol has a schedule in which it never decides. The proof finds a bivalent configuration, from which both decisions are still reachable, and shows that an adversary who only chooses the order of delivery can always keep it bivalent.

The conference version appeared in 1983; this is the journal version. Every practical consensus protocol escapes by weakening an assumption: randomization (Ben-Or), partial synchrony (Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer), or probabilistic finality (Nakamoto).

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.randomized.ben_or() with a deterministic coin shows one such stalling schedule: without its coins, Ben-Or’s protocol is deterministic again and falls under the theorem.

References: M. J. Fischer, N. A. Lynch, and M. S. Paterson, Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process, Journal of the ACM 32(2), 374–382 (1985). DOI.

FLP: no deterministic consensus in an asynchronous network (1985)

FLP: no deterministic consensus in an asynchronous network (1985)

1988 – Partial Synchrony: Progress after an Unknown Stabilization Time#

Networks are usually fast and sometimes slow. Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer introduced partially synchronous models, in which a delay bound \(\Delta\) exists but is unknown, or holds only after an unknown global stabilization time (GST), and showed that consensus is then solvable for \(n \ge 3f + 1\) Byzantine faults. The design separates safety from progress. Safety rests on overlapping quorums, never on timing, so the protocol stays safe even while the network misbehaves. Progress needs a leader whose messages arrive before the others give up on it. Each view’s leader gets a timeout that doubles after every failed view, \(T_v = T_0\,2^{v}\). Views keep starting and timeouts grow without bound, so some view eventually starts after GST with a timeout longer than the delay:

\[\exists\, v:\ t_v \ge \text{GST} \ \wedge\ T_v \ge \Delta.\]

Rotating the leader among the views that follow reaches an honest one within \(f + 1\) views. Progress is guaranteed without ever knowing \(\Delta\). PBFT, Tendermint and HotStuff all assume this model.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.synchrony.view_changes().

References: C. Dwork, N. Lynch, and L. Stockmeyer, Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony, Journal of the ACM 35(2), 288–323 (1988). DOI.

Partial synchrony: timeouts that eventually work (Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer 1988)

Partial synchrony: timeouts that eventually work (Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer 1988)

1992 – Pricing via Processing#

To fight junk mail, Dwork and Naor proposed that every message should cost its sender a little computation: too little to bother an ordinary user, too much for someone sending millions, and instantly checkable by the recipient. Their pricing functions are moderately hard to compute, easy to verify, and cannot be amortized across messages. One example is the modular square root, for \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\):

\[r = x^{(p+1)/4} \bmod p \quad (\approx 1.5 \log_2 p \text{ multiplications}), \qquad r^2 \equiv x \pmod p \quad (1 \text{ multiplication}).\]

The gap between computing and checking is only a factor of about \(\log_2 p\). Raising the price means enlarging \(p\), which also makes the verifier’s multiplication more expensive. Later designs therefore moved to puzzles whose cost grows exponentially in a tunable parameter while checking stays a single cheap step. The idea of attaching a verifiable computational cost is the root of proof of work.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.pricing.modular_square_root() counts its multiplications.

References: C. Dwork and M. Naor, Pricing via Processing or Combatting Junk Mail, CRYPTO ‘92, LNCS 740, 139–147 (1993). DOI.

Pricing via processing: making junk mail expensive (Dwork and Naor 1992)

Pricing via processing: making junk mail expensive (Dwork and Naor 1992)

1997-2002 – Hashcash and Proofs of Computational Effort#

Adam Back’s Hashcash (proposed in 1997, described in a 2002 paper) priced requests with a hash puzzle: the sender varies a nonce until the hash meets a target, and the recipient checks the answer with a single hash. With \(d\) required leading zero bits, each attempt succeeds with probability \(2^{-d}\):

\[H(\mathrm{header})\leq 2^{256-d}-1,\qquad \Pr[\mathrm{success}]=2^{-d},\qquad \mathbb{E}[T]=2^d.\]

Here \(T\) counts attempts. The cost is tunable and exponential in \(d\). Each attempt is an independent trial, so \(T\) is geometric and any one search can finish much earlier or later than the average. Bitcoin’s proof of work is Hashcash applied to block headers. Because the trials are memoryless, blocks across the whole network arrive as a Poisson process, which the 2008 double-spend calculation relies on.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.pow.mine() searches a bounded nonce interval; blockchainkit.consensus.systems.pow.valid_pow() checks one hash. Difficulty counts leading zero bits of SHA-256 over this package’s header; Bitcoin compares the double SHA-256 of an 80-byte header with a full 256-bit target.

References: A. Back, Hashcash — A Denial of Service Counter-Measure (2002), describing the earlier 1997 proposal. Author’s paper.

Hashcash: proof of work you can verify in one hash (Back 1997)

Hashcash: proof of work you can verify in one hash (Back 1997)

1999 – Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance#

Castro and Liskov made Byzantine fault tolerance practical: a group of \(3f + 1\) replicas keeps one consistent log even if \(f\) of them lie, using a three-phase protocol (pre-prepare, prepare, commit) and view changes for faulty leaders. A replica cannot wait for all \(3f + 1\) votes, because \(f\) faulty replicas may stay silent, so it acts on a quorum of the \(2f + 1\) it can count on. Safety then rests on quorum intersection:

\[|Q_1 \cap Q_2| \ \ge\ 2(2f + 1) - (3f + 1) = f + 1.\]

Two quorums share at least \(f + 1\) replicas, so at least one of them is honest. That replica never votes for two values in one view, so two conflicting values can never both gather a quorum. With more than \(f\) faults that guarantee disappears. Tendermint, HotStuff and many proof-of-stake chains descend from PBFT.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.pbft.pbft_round() runs one normal-case round with a fixed leader and synchronous delivery, with blockchainkit.consensus.systems.pbft.quorum_size(). It has no view changes, checkpoints, request batching or message authentication.

References: M. Castro and B. Liskov, Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, 3rd Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation (OSDI), 173–186 (1999).

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (Castro and Liskov 1999)

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (Castro and Liskov 1999)

2008-2009 – Nakamoto Consensus: Bitcoin Combines the Ingredients#

Alice could sign two payments that each spend her entire balance: both signatures are valid, but both payments cannot be accepted. Nakamoto’s 2008 proposal solved this double-spending problem without a trusted party by combining earlier ingredients: signatures, hash-linked blocks, proof of work, and peer-to-peer gossip. Peers accept the valid chain with the most cumulative work, and switch when a heavier one appears:

\[\text{tip} = \arg\max_{\text{valid chains } C} \sum_{b \in C} \text{work}(b).\]

Work cannot be faked, so to replace a block an attacker must redo it and then outpace the rest of the network. With less than half the hashrate this is the losing gambler of 1656. An earlier payment can still be undone by a reorganization, so no finite number of confirmations gives unconditional finality; the 2008 entry below computes how fast the risk falls.

Implementation: blockchainkit.structures.systems.chain.Blockchain validates account transfers and selects the greatest cumulative work. Each fork keeps its own blockchainkit.structures.systems.ledger.Ledger, so a reorganization restores balances and account nonces, and equal-work ties go to the smaller hash so that experiments are reproducible. Difficulty is fixed by genesis, and there are no UTXOs, coinbase rewards, fees, scripts, or wire protocol: fork choice by cumulative work is the part reproduced.

References: S. Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System (2008). Original announcement; paper.

Nakamoto consensus: a payment, a fork, and a reorganization (2008)

Nakamoto consensus: a payment, a fork, and a reorganization (2008)

2008 – How Many Confirmations? Nakamoto’s Double-Spend Calculation#

A merchant who waits for more blocks on top of a payment is safer, because an attacker must secretly build a longer chain. Section 11 of the whitepaper computes how much safer, for an attacker with hashrate share \(q\) (and \(p = 1 - q\) for the honest network) and a merchant waiting for \(z\) confirmations. The attacker mines at \(q/p\) times the honest rate, so while the honest network finds \(z\) blocks the attacker’s progress \(k\) is Poisson with mean \(\lambda = zq/p\). An attacker with \(k \le z\) blocks still trails by \(z - k\) and, by the gambler’s ruin, catches up with probability \((q/p)^{z-k}\); one with more blocks has already won. Subtracting the chance of failure from one:

\[P(q, z) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{z} \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \left(1 - (q/p)^{z-k}\right).\]

The whitepaper tabulates \(P\) (for example 0.0009137 at \(q = 0.1\), \(z = 5\)) and the \(z\) needed for \(P < 0.1\%\). Later work refined the model (Rosenfeld 2014; Grunspan and Pérez-Marco 2017), but the exponential decay remains.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.catch_up.attacker_success_probability() reproduces the whitepaper’s table.

References: S. Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System (2008), section 11. Paper.

How many confirmations? Nakamoto’s double-spend calculation (2008)

How many confirmations? Nakamoto's double-spend calculation (2008)

2009 – Difficulty Retargeting#

If more miners join, blocks come faster. The whitepaper says difficulty is set by a moving average targeting an average number of blocks per hour, and Bitcoin’s first release (January 2009) recomputes the target \(T\) every 2016 blocks, scaled by how long they actually took and clamped to a factor of four:

\[T' = T \cdot \frac{\min\!\bigl(\max(t_{\text{actual}}, t_{\text{expected}}/4),\ 4\,t_{\text{expected}}\bigr)}{t_{\text{expected}}}.\]

A larger target is easier to hit, so blocks that came too slowly raise \(T\) and blocks that came too fast lower it. The clamp limits manipulation and shocks. Retargeting turns proof of work into a clock that returns to about ten minutes per block whatever the hashrate. It also matters for selfish mining (2014 entry below). Orphaned blocks slow the chain, so retargeting lowers the difficulty, and a pool that holds a larger share of the chain then also earns more blocks per hour.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.difficulty.retarget() and blockchainkit.consensus.systems.difficulty.simulate_difficulty() use Bitcoin’s clamp on an integer target, in a simulation with given hashrates; they do not reproduce the off-by-one in Bitcoin’s interval count.

References: S. Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System (2008), section 4; Bitcoin v0.1 source code (2009), GetNextWorkRequired.

Difficulty retargeting: keeping ten-minute blocks (Bitcoin 2009)

Difficulty retargeting: keeping ten-minute blocks (Bitcoin 2009)

2012 – Peercoin: Stake as an Alternative to Work#

King and Nadal’s Peercoin explored proof of stake alongside proof of work: the right to propose blocks could be weighted by coins held rather than by computation performed. Coins are as scarce as computation, so an attacker still needs a large share of a limited resource, but using them burns no electricity. The basic building block is a weighted lottery, in which twice the stake gives twice the chance of being selected:

\[\Pr[\text{proposer}=i]=\frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}.\]

A lottery alone is not consensus: a proof-of-stake protocol still needs an unbiasable source of randomness, voting, and penalties, which the following entries add.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.pos.StakeSampler samples integer weights exactly, from a private seeded generator. It does not model Peercoin’s coin age, voting, slashing or finality; deployed protocols derive randomness with RANDAO or verifiable random functions and must resist validators grinding it.

References: S. King and S. Nadal, PPCoin: Peer-to-Peer Crypto-Currency with Proof-of-Stake (2012). Project paper.

Proof of stake: a stake-weighted proposer lottery (Peercoin 2012)

Proof of stake: a stake-weighted proposer lottery (Peercoin 2012)

2013 – GHOST: The Heaviest Observed Subtree#

When blocks are frequent, honest miners often build on the same parent at once, and the longest-chain rule throws their extra blocks away. Sompolinsky and Zohar showed that this degrades security at high block rates. An attacker’s private chain has no forks, so all its work counts, while honest work split across sibling blocks only partly lengthens the main chain. An attacker can therefore win with less than half the hashrate. GHOST (Greedy Heaviest Observed SubTree) counts the work in sibling blocks too. It descends from genesis, at each fork choosing

\[\text{child} = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{b \in \text{subtree}(c)} \text{work}(b).\]

Sibling blocks still add weight to their common branch. Without forks, GHOST picks the same tip as the longest-chain rule. Ethereum’s proof-of-work chain adopted GHOST’s uncle rewards but kept heaviest-chain selection; a GHOST variant, LMD-GHOST, became its fork-choice rule with proof of stake.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.fork_choice.ghost_tip() and blockchainkit.consensus.systems.fork_choice.subtree_work() apply GHOST to a static block tree.

References: Y. Sompolinsky and A. Zohar, Secure High-Rate Transaction Processing in Bitcoin, Financial Cryptography 2015, LNCS 8975, 507–527 (2015); preprint IACR ePrint 2013/881. DOI.

GHOST: follow the heaviest subtree (Sompolinsky and Zohar 2013)

GHOST: follow the heaviest subtree (Sompolinsky and Zohar 2013)

2014 – Nothing at Stake, and Slashing#

A proof-of-work miner must spend electricity on one fork or another. In early 2014 critics pointed out that a proof-of-stake validator can sign every fork for free and collect the reward whichever wins, so forks never settle. Buterin’s Slasher answered with penalties: evidence of two signatures at the same height destroys the validator’s deposit. Take reward \(R\) for voting on the winning fork, probability \(p_A\) that fork \(A\) wins, and penalty \(S\), and assume that a double vote is always caught. Voting only for \(A\) pays when \(A\) wins, and voting for both always pays but is always punished:

\[E[\text{vote } A] = p_A R, \qquad E[\text{vote both}] = R - S,\]

so voting for one fork is the best response once \(S > (1 - p_A) R\). Slashing is now part of every major proof-of-stake protocol. It works only while the deposit is still locked. Keys whose deposits were already withdrawn can sign an alternative history at no cost, which is the long-range attack.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.stake_games.fork_voting_payoffs() is a payoff table, not a protocol; real slashing needs on-chain evidence and deposits that are still locked.

References: V. Buterin, Slasher: A Punitive Proof-of-Stake Algorithm, Ethereum blog (January 2014). Post.

Nothing at stake: why proof of stake needs penalties (Buterin 2014)

Nothing at stake: why proof of stake needs penalties (Buterin 2014)

2014 – Selfish Mining: Majority Is Not Enough#

Eyal and Sirer showed that a large pool can earn more than its fair share by withholding the blocks it finds and releasing them just in time to orphan honest blocks. The pool sometimes loses its own blocks too, but honest miners lose more, so the pool’s fraction of the final chain rises. They modelled the strategy as a Markov chain on the pool’s private lead and derived its share of the chain, for hashrate share \(\alpha\) and fraction \(\gamma\) of honest miners that build on the pool’s block during a tie:

\[R(\alpha, \gamma) = \frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)^2\bigl(4\alpha + \gamma(1-2\alpha)\bigr) - \alpha^3}{1 - \alpha\bigl(1 + (2-\alpha)\alpha\bigr)}.\]

This exceeds \(\alpha\) whenever \(\alpha > (1-\gamma)/(3-2\gamma)\): a third of the hashrate when ties go to honest blocks, and any share when the pool wins every tie. Honest miners then gain by joining the pool, so Bitcoin’s incentive argument needs more than an honest majority.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.selfish.selfish_mining_revenue(), blockchainkit.consensus.systems.selfish.selfish_mining_threshold(), and the state-machine simulation blockchainkit.consensus.systems.selfish.simulate_selfish_mining(), in Eyal and Sirer’s idealized model: propagation is instant apart from the tie share \(\gamma\), and difficulty does not adjust.

References: I. Eyal and E. G. Sirer, Majority Is Not Enough: Bitcoin Mining Is Vulnerable, Financial Cryptography 2014, LNCS 8437, 436–454 (2014). DOI.

Selfish mining: majority is not enough (Eyal and Sirer 2014)

Selfish mining: majority is not enough (Eyal and Sirer 2014)

2017 – Casper FFG: Finality from Two-Thirds Votes#

Buterin and Griffith’s Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget overlays finality on any block-proposal mechanism. Validators vote for links \(s \to t\) from a source checkpoint to a later target. A checkpoint is justified when two thirds of the stake vote for a link to it from a justified checkpoint, and it is final when the link that justifies its direct successor starts from it. Two slashing conditions forbid, for one validator, two distinct votes for the same target height and a vote that surrounds another:

\[h(t_1) = h(t_2) \ \text{for distinct votes}, \qquad h(s_1) < h(s_2) < h(t_2) < h(t_1).\]

They prove accountable safety: two conflicting finalized checkpoints imply that validators holding at least a third of the stake violated a condition and can be punished. The argument is PBFT’s quorum intersection. Two sets that each hold two thirds of the stake overlap in at least a third, and the validators in the overlap cannot have voted for both checkpoints without breaking one of the rules. Where PBFT assumes the overlap contains an honest replica, Casper makes it identifiable and punishable. Ethereum’s proof of stake combines Casper FFG with LMD-GHOST.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.finality.FinalityGadget tracks justification, finality and offenses for a fixed validator set. It has no deposits, rewards, inactivity leak or fork choice.

References: V. Buterin and V. Griffith, Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget, arXiv:1710.09437 (2017). Preprint.

Casper FFG: finality by two-thirds votes and slashing (Buterin and Griffith 2017)

Casper FFG: finality by two-thirds votes and slashing (Buterin and Griffith 2017)

2017 – Algorand’s Cryptographic Sortition#

Gilad, Hemo, Micali, Vlachos and Zeldovich built Algorand around a private lottery: each round’s committee is drawn so that every unit of stake is a ticket, and each user checks their own tickets with a verifiable random function on the round seed. A user with stake \(w\), out of total stake \(W\) and expected committee size \(\tau\), holds \(j\) seats with

\[j \sim \text{Binomial}\!\left(w, \frac{\tau}{W}\right), \qquad E[j] = \tau\,\frac{w}{W}.\]

The function needs the user’s secret key, so nobody else knows who is on the committee until its members speak and attach their proofs. They therefore cannot be attacked in advance, and Algorand replaces the committee after every step, so attacking a member after it speaks is too late. Independent binomials with the same probability add, so splitting stake across accounts leaves the distribution of seats unchanged: there is no Sybil advantage.

Implementation: blockchainkit.consensus.systems.sortition.sortition(). A hash of the user’s secret replaces the verifiable random function, so others cannot check a claimed draw.

References: Y. Gilad, R. Hemo, S. Micali, G. Vlachos, and N. Zeldovich, Algorand: Scaling Byzantine Agreements for Cryptocurrencies, 26th Symposium on Operating Systems Principles (SOSP), 51–68 (2017). DOI.

Cryptographic sortition: a secret, stake-weighted committee (Algorand 2017)

Cryptographic sortition: a secret, stake-weighted committee (Algorand 2017)
