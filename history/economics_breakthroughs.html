Breakthroughs in Blockchain Economics#

blockchainkit.economics hub · History · Examples · API reference

“It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.” – Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776

Cryptography makes cheating detectable. Incentives make honesty the best course: the rewards and penalties are set so that following the rules pays more than breaking them. This page is about those incentives.

Some vocabulary first.

  • A blockchain is a shared record of payments. Think of a Python list of blocks, where each block holds a batch of transactions: signed instructions such as “pay Bob 5 coins”. Thousands of computers keep copies of the list in step, without trusting each other.

  • Consensus is the set of rules by which those computers agree on which block comes next.

  • In proof of work (Bitcoin), miners add blocks. They race to solve a brute-force puzzle: hash candidate blocks until a hash falls below a target. A miner’s hashrate is how many guesses it makes per second. Its chance of finding the next block equals its share of the total hashrate.

  • In proof of stake (Ethereum since 2022), validators add blocks. Each locks up coins as a stake, and the protocol picks one at random, weighted by stake, to propose each block.

  • Whoever adds a block is paid for it. Users also pay fees to get their transactions into a block, because each block has limited space.

  • A smart contract, or just contract, is a program stored on the blockchain. It can hold coins and runs when a transaction calls it. It is like a Python object whose methods anyone can call but whose code nobody can change. A token is a coin defined by such a contract. Ether (ETH) is Ethereum’s own coin.

A blockchain pays the miners and validators who secure it. It sells its scarce block space to the users who want it. It also hosts markets whose prices and ordering can be exploited. Breakthroughs in Consensus covers two incentive attacks on consensus itself, selfish mining and nothing at stake; Breakthroughs in Smart Contracts covers the flash loan as a contract. This chronology follows the economics:

  • the game theory and auction theory the field borrowed;

  • how block rewards and fees pay for security;

  • how fee markets price block space;

  • how decentralized exchanges and stablecoins work;

  • how the order of transactions became a market of its own.

The models in blockchainkit.economics are pure functions and small seeded simulations. The stablecoin, the exchange and the lender are contracts on World. Exact conventions and model boundaries lists where they depart from deployed systems.

1960 – Schelling’s Focal Points: Coordinating without Communicating#

Two people must meet somewhere in New York. They cannot talk to agree on a place or a time. How can they end up together?

Game theory studies situations like this one, called games. Each player picks a strategy, meaning a choice of what to do. Each player’s payoff depends on everyone’s choices. An equilibrium is a set of choices in which no player can do better by changing only their own choice.

In The Strategy of Conflict, Schelling asked people this very question. Any place and time is an equilibrium: if both choose it, neither gains by switching. So the mathematics alone does not pick an answer. Yet most people chose the same one: noon at Grand Central Station. A focal point is an equilibrium that stands out, by convention, uniqueness or salience (being the obvious one to notice). Players reach it because each expects the others to.

To see why salience matters, model it with \(k\) players and \(n\) options. Each player chooses independently:

  1. With probability \(s\), the player picks the salient option.

  2. Otherwise, with probability \(1-s\), the player picks uniformly among all \(n\) options, the salient one included, like random.choice(options).

The salient option is then chosen with probability \(s + (1-s)/n\) and each other one with \((1-s)/n\). All players coincide with probability

\[P = \left(s + \frac{1-s}{n}\right)^k + (n - 1)\left(\frac{1-s}{n}\right)^k.\]

In words: the first term is the chance that everyone lands on the salient option. The second adds the chance that everyone lands on the same one of the other \(n - 1\) options.

  • Without salience (\(s = 0\)), \(P = n^{1-k}\). Each extra player divides the chance of agreement by \(n\). Ten options and five players give one chance in 10,000.

  • With salience, each extra player multiplies it by only about \(s + (1-s)/n\). With \(s = 0.9\) and the same ten options and five players, \(P \approx 0.91^5 \approx 0.62\).

  • So a strongly salient answer keeps even a large group coordinated. Blockchain oracles and voting games rely on the truth being that salient answer.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.coordination.coordination_probability() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.coordination.play_coordination(). Salience is a single probability shared by every player; Schelling’s experiments measured it rather than assuming it.

References: T. C. Schelling, The Strategy of Conflict, Harvard University Press (1960), chapter 3.

1961 – Vickrey’s Second-Price Auction and Truthful Bidding#

In a sealed-bid auction, each bidder writes down one secret bid and the highest bid wins. In a first-price auction, the winner pays its own bid. Each bidder has a value: the most the item is worth to it. Bidding that true value guarantees zero profit, even when you win. So every bidder shades its bid: it bids below its value. By how much depends on what it guesses the others will bid, and a wrong guess costs money.

Vickrey changed one rule. The winner pays the second-highest bid instead. A bid then decides only whether you win, never the price you pay.

  1. Each bidder \(i\) submits a sealed bid \(b_i\).

  2. The highest bid wins.

  3. The winner pays the highest of the other bids, \(\max_{j \ne i} b_j\), like max(b for j, b in bids.items() if j != i).

Economists measure a bidder’s gain by its utility, here simply its profit in money. The bidder with value \(v_i\) has utility

\[\begin{split}u_i(b_i) = \begin{cases} v_i - \max_{j \ne i} b_j & \text{if } b_i > \max_{j \ne i} b_j,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\end{split}\]

In words: if your bid beats every other bid, your profit is your value minus the best rival bid. Otherwise you get nothing and pay nothing.

Why bidding your true value \(b_i = v_i\) is best:

  • It wins exactly when the price \(\max_{j \ne i} b_j\) is below \(v_i\), that is, exactly when winning is profitable.

  • Bidding more adds only wins at a price above \(v_i\). Those lose money.

  • Bidding less gives up only wins at a price below \(v_i\). Those would have made money.

For example, take \(v_i = 10\). If the best rival bids 7, bidding 10 wins and earns 3, while bidding 6 loses and earns 0. If the best rival bids 12, bidding 10 loses and earns 0, while bidding 13 wins and earns \(-2\).

So truthful bidding is optimal whatever the others do. Game theorists call this a dominant strategy: a choice that is best no matter what the others choose. A mechanism is a set of rules that turns everyone’s reports into an outcome and payments, like a function from bids to a winner and a price. Mechanisms that make honest reporting optimal are the template for fee mechanisms and builder auctions on blockchains.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.second_price_auction() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.first_price_auction(), whose AuctionResult reports each bidder’s utility. Bids are sealed by fiat; on a public blockchain they must be hidden by commit-reveal or encryption, or the auction is not sealed at all.

References: W. Vickrey, Counterspeculation, auctions, and competitive sealed tenders, The Journal of Finance 16(1), 8–37 (1961). DOI.

1981 – Myerson’s Optimal Auctions and Revenue Equivalence#

Myerson asked which auction gives the seller the most expected revenue: the average it earns if the auction were run many times. Each bidder’s value is private: only the bidder knows it. The seller knows only how values are spread, as a probability distribution.

  • \(F(v)\) is the probability that a value is at most \(v\) (the cumulative distribution function).

  • \(f(v) = F'(v)\) is its density, the height of the histogram at \(v\).

  • For values uniform on \([0, h]\), \(F(v) = v/h\) and \(f(v) = 1/h\).

The seller cannot charge each bidder its value. A bidder would then pretend to value the item less. To keep bidders honest, the seller must leave each one some profit, called an information rent: the bidder earns it only because it alone knows its value. Myerson accounted for this rent by replacing each value with its virtual value:

\[\varphi(v) = v - \frac{1 - F(v)}{f(v)},\]

In words: the virtual value is the value minus a correction for the rent the seller must leave. For the uniform case it is \(\varphi(v) = 2v - h\).

Myerson showed that expected revenue equals the expected virtual value of the winner. Maximizing revenue therefore means selling to the bidder with the highest virtual value, and not selling at all when every virtual value is negative. A distribution is regular when the virtual value rises with the value. For identical bidders with a regular distribution, the best auction runs as follows:

  1. The seller announces a reserve price \(r = \varphi^{-1}(0)\), the value whose virtual value is zero. It is \(h/2\) for values uniform on \([0, h]\).

  2. Each bidder submits a sealed bid. Bids below \(r\) are ignored.

  3. If no bid reaches \(r\), the item stays unsold.

  4. Otherwise the highest bid wins and pays the larger of \(r\) and the second-highest bid.

That is a second-price auction with a reserve price. With values uniform on \([0, 100]\), the reserve is 50. Selling to a lone bidder at 40 would earn less on average than holding out for 50.

The same identity gives revenue equivalence:

  • Take two auctions that give the item to the same bidders, and in which a bidder with the lowest possible value pays nothing.

  • They raise the same expected revenue.

  • So a first-price auction with shaded bids earns what a second-price one earns with truthful bids. With two bidders uniform on \([0, h]\), bidders in the first-price auction bid half their value, and both auctions earn \(h/3\) on average.

Blockchain fee mechanisms, from EIP-1559’s base fee to builder auctions (both later on this page), are analyzed with these tools.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.virtual_value(), blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.optimal_reserve(), blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.equilibrium_bid(), blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.expected_revenue() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.auctions.simulate_revenue(). Values are independent and uniform on \([0, h]\), the case with closed forms; Myerson’s result covers any regular distribution.

References: R. B. Myerson, Optimal auction design, Mathematics of Operations Research 6(1), 58–73 (1981). DOI.

2003 – Hanson’s Logarithmic Market Scoring Rule#

A prediction market sells shares in possible outcomes, such as “it rains tomorrow”. Each share pays 1 if its outcome happens and 0 otherwise. A trader who believes an outcome has probability \(p\) buys its shares while they cost less than \(p\), since each then pays back more than it costs on average. So prices read as the market’s probabilities.

But a market with few traders has an empty order book: the list of standing offers to buy and sell, like a dict from price to quantity waiting to be matched. A trader with information then finds nobody to trade with.

Hanson proposed an automated market maker. A market maker is a trader who always stands ready to buy or sell. Hanson’s is a program that always quotes a price, using a formula instead of a human.

  1. The market maker stores \(q_i\), the number of shares sold of each of \(n\) outcomes, as a list q.

  2. A trader asks to buy shares, which changes q to a new list q2.

  3. The trader pays C(q2) - C(q), the change in a cost function \(C\).

  4. When the outcome \(w\) is known, the market maker pays 1 per winning share, \(q_w\) in total.

The price of each outcome is the derivative of \(C\): how fast the cost grows per extra share of that outcome.

\[C(q) = b \ln \sum_i e^{q_i/b}, \qquad p_i = \frac{\partial C}{\partial q_i} = \frac{e^{q_i/b}}{\sum_j e^{q_j/b}}.\]

In words: \(C\) is a smooth version of max(q), always between max(q) and max(q) + b * log(n). The prices are the softmax of \(q/b\), [exp(qi / b) / sum(exp(qj / b) for qj in q) for qi in q].

  • Prices behave like probabilities. They are positive and sum to one.

  • The market maker’s loss is bounded. It starts at \(C(0) = b \ln n\) and finally pays \(q_w\) for the winning outcome \(w\). Since \(C(q) \ge q_w\), the traders have paid it \(C(q) - C(0) \ge q_w - b \ln n\). Its loss is therefore at most \(b \ln n\). For two outcomes and \(b = 100\), that is about 69. This bounded subsidy buys information.

  • The parameter \(b\) sets the liquidity. Liquidity is how much you can trade without moving the price much. A larger \(b\) means each trade moves the price less, but the maximum loss is larger.

On-chain prediction markets adopted it. It is also the ancestor of the formula-priced pools of decentralized exchanges: exchanges run by smart contracts instead of companies.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.lmsr.lmsr_cost(), blockchainkit.economics.systems.lmsr.lmsr_prices(), blockchainkit.economics.systems.lmsr.lmsr_trade() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.lmsr.lmsr_max_loss(), in floating point; a contract would use fixed-point arithmetic, for which the exponential is costly.

References: R. Hanson, Combinatorial information market design, Information Systems Frontiers 5(1), 107–119 (2003).

2008 – Nakamoto’s Incentive: Block Rewards and Fees#

Bitcoin’s miners are strangers. Why should they follow the rules?

Section 6 of the Bitcoin paper pays the creator of each block in two ways: new coins, called the block reward or subsidy, and the fees of the block’s transactions. The reward does three jobs:

  • it distributes the currency;

  • it pays for the work that secures the chain;

  • Nakamoto argued, it keeps even a powerful miner honest: such a miner “ought to find it more profitable to play by the rules.”

The argument compares two uses of the same hashrate. The dishonest use is a double spend: paying someone and then erasing the payment. It relies on the rule that everyone follows the longest chain. A merchant waits for \(z\) confirmations, meaning \(z\) blocks built on top of the payment, before handing over the goods.

  1. The attacker pays a merchant \(V\).

  2. The merchant waits for \(z\) confirmations and hands over the goods.

  3. Meanwhile, the attacker secretly mines a private chain that leaves out the payment. It needs about \(m = z + 1\) blocks to overtake the public chain.

  4. If the private chain becomes longer, the attacker publishes it. Everyone switches to it, the payment vanishes, and the attacker keeps both the goods and the coins.

Mined honestly, those \(m\) blocks earn \(mR\) for a reward \(R\) per block. In the attack they earn nothing unless the private chain wins. That happens with the whitepaper’s catch-up probability \(P_z\): the chance that an attacker starting \(z\) blocks behind ever draws level. If it wins, the attacker keeps \(V\) as well as its blocks’ rewards. But a fraction \(d\) of those rewards has lost its worth, because the attack shook confidence in the coin. Attacking pays when

\[P_z \left(V + (1 - d)\, m R\right) > m R .\]

In words: the attacker’s expected gain from attacking (left) beats the sure income from mining honestly (right).

  • A small miner rarely catches up, so \(P_z\) is tiny and only a payment worth many blocks tempts it. With 10% of the hashrate and \(z = 6\), the whitepaper gives \(P_z \approx 0.00024\). The payment would then have to be worth tens of thousands of block rewards.

  • A majority miner always catches up, \(P_z = 1\), and the condition becomes \(V > d\, m R\). It is deterred only by the loss \(d\) on “the validity of his own wealth.”

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.rewards.block_reward() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.rewards.double_spend_incentive(), using attacker_success_probability(). The comparison counts the attacker’s own blocks, not time, and ignores propagation delay and the cost of the hardware.

References: S. Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A peer-to-peer electronic cash system (2008), sections 6 and 11.

2009 – Fixed Supply and the Halving Schedule#

How many coins should exist, and who creates them? The first Bitcoin release fixed this monetary policy in code.

Two terms:

  • A block’s height \(h\) is its position in the chain, its index in the list. The first block, the genesis block, has height 0.

  • A satoshi is the smallest unit, \(10^{-8}\) bitcoin. Amounts are stored as integers of satoshis, the way prices are often stored in cents.

The block at height \(h\) creates

\[S(h) = \left\lfloor 50 \cdot 10^8 / 2^{\lfloor h/210\,000 \rfloor} \right\rfloor \text{ satoshis},\]

that is, start at 50 bitcoin and halve every 210,000 blocks, rounding down. \(\lfloor x \rfloor\) is rounding down, // in Python. The whole formula is (50 * 10**8) >> (h // 210_000).

  • Blocks 0 to 209,999 create 50 bitcoin each.

  • Blocks 210,000 to 419,999 create 25 each.

  • And so on, halving about every four years at ten minutes a block.

Each period issues half as many coins as the one before. So the total approaches the geometric sum \(210\,000 \times 50 \times (1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \cdots) = 21\) million coins.

The rounding down drops fractions of a satoshi at every halving, so the total falls slightly short. The subsidy, \(5 \times 10^9\) satoshis at first, reaches zero at the 33rd halving, around 2140, because \(2^{33} > 5 \times 10^9\). By then 20,999,999.9769 coins are issued.

Consequences:

  • A fixed, predictable supply. This was a deliberate contrast with central-bank money, which a central bank can create when it chooses.

  • Fees must take over. Miners must eventually be paid by fees alone.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.rewards.block_subsidy(), the logic of Bitcoin Core’s GetBlockSubsidy, and blockchainkit.economics.systems.rewards.issued_supply(), which counts the genesis block’s 50 coins although Bitcoin can never spend them.

References: S. Nakamoto, Bitcoin v0.1 source code, main.cpp, GetBlockValue (January 2009); Bitcoin Core, validation.cpp, GetBlockSubsidy.

2014 – SchellingCoin and Decentralized Oracles#

Contracts cannot see the world. They do not know the price of ether in dollars, or who won an election. Someone must report such facts to them. A service that does this is an oracle. A single reporter could simply lie.

Buterin’s SchellingCoin asks many reporters and pays them for agreeing with each other.

  1. Each reporter locks a deposit \(D\) in the contract.

  2. Each reporter submits a report \(r_i\).

  3. The contract publishes the median report as the answer: the middle value of the sorted reports.

  4. It pays a reward \(R\) to each reporter whose report lies between the lower and upper quartiles, the values a quarter and three quarters of the way along the sorted reports (roughly sorted(r)[len(r) // 4] and sorted(r)[3 * len(r) // 4]). Every other reporter loses its deposit.

\[\begin{split}\text{payout}_i = \begin{cases} R & q_{1/4} \le r_i \le q_{3/4},\\ -D & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\end{split}\]

In words: reports in the middle half earn \(R\); reports outside it lose \(D\).

Why it works:

  • A reporter is paid for agreeing with the others. So its best report is the one it expects the others to make.

  • Each reporter knows the truth, and expects the others to know it. So the truth is the focal point: if everyone else reports it, reporting it too is best.

Its limits:

  • It is only as honest as its majority. A cartel, a group that secretly coordinates, can report one false value.

    • Above a quarter of the reports, the cartel is paid, since its value then reaches the quartile.

    • Above half, it sets the median.

    • Above three quarters, it pushes the honest reports outside the quartiles and takes their deposits.

  • A bribe that is never paid can move the focal point. This is Buterin’s later P + epsilon attack:

    1. The attacker publicly promises a reward to everyone who reports a false answer \(X\), payable only if \(X\) loses. The reward is slightly more than they would have earned by reporting the truth.

    2. Now reporting \(X\) is the safer choice for each reporter. If \(X\) wins, they are paid by the scheme. If \(X\) loses, they are paid by the attacker.

    3. So everyone reports \(X\), \(X\) wins, and the promise costs nothing.

UMA’s and Kleros’s dispute resolution build on the same idea.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.oracles.schelling_round(), which settles one round; there are no rounds over time, no commit-reveal of reports, and no token whose value depends on the oracle’s honesty.

References: V. Buterin, SchellingCoin: a minimal-trust universal data feed, Ethereum Blog (March 2014); V. Buterin, The P + epsilon attack, Ethereum Blog (January 2015).

2016 – Carlsten et al.: the Instability of Bitcoin without the Block Reward#

Once the subsidy is gone, a block earns only the fees it collects. What does that do to miners?

Pending transactions wait in the mempool, a pool of unconfirmed transactions that each miner keeps, like a dict from transaction id to transaction. Fees arrive in the mempool at a steady rate. Blocks, however, arrive at random: finding one is a lottery, with one win every ten minutes on average. So a block’s reward is proportional to the time since the last one, and a block found right after another holds almost nothing. Carlsten, Kalodner, Weinberg and Narayanan showed two consequences.

First, the mining gap. \(t\) seconds after a block, the next one is worth \(S + ft\) for a subsidy \(S\) and a fee rate \(f\). Let \(c\) be what a miner spends on electricity per block it expects to find. While \(S + ft < c\), mining loses money, so miners pause for a mining gap of

\[t^* = \max\left(0, \frac{c - S}{f}\right)\]

seconds. In words: the time it takes fees to pile up enough to cover the cost the subsidy does not. It is zero while the subsidy covers the cost. While miners pause, less hashrate protects the chain.

Second, undercutting. A fork is a split in the chain: two competing blocks at the same height, like two branches in git. The tip is the latest block of the chain. A miner can fork the tip instead of extending it.

  1. The tip has just claimed more fees than remain in the mempool.

  2. A miner re-mines the tip’s height, in a rival block that claims the tip’s fees as well as the pending ones. The fork offers more than building on the tip.

  3. By undercutting, the miner claims only part of the fees in its own block. It leaves the rest for the next block.

  4. That makes its fork the more lucrative branch for other miners to build on. If they do, the original tip is dropped and its miner loses its reward.

They also showed that selfish mining becomes profitable for arbitrarily small miners. Selfish mining means hiding found blocks and releasing them at the best moment, to waste other miners’ work.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.fee_only.simulate_block_rewards(), blockchainkit.economics.systems.fee_only.mining_gap() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.fee_only.undercutting_payoffs(). The undercutting model is a one-shot comparison in which other miners follow the more lucrative branch; the paper simulates repeated play with learning miners.

References: M. Carlsten, H. Kalodner, S. M. Weinberg and A. Narayanan, On the instability of Bitcoin without the block reward, Proc. ACM CCS 2016, 154–167 (2016).

2016 – Kiayias et al.: Mining Games and When Honest Mining Is an Equilibrium#

Is mining honestly always a miner’s best choice?

Kiayias, Koutsoupias, Kyropoulou and Tselekounis modeled mining as a stochastic game: a game played over many rounds, in which chance (who finds the next block) moves the players between states. Each miner chooses which block to extend and when to publish. A best response is the best strategy given what the others do.

  • When every miner’s hashrate is small, honest mining is a best response to honest mining.

  • A large miner gains by deviating, and other equilibria arise.

The same year, Sapirshtein, Sompolinsky and Zohar computed a miner’s best deviation exactly. Their model:

  1. A miner has a share \(\alpha\) of the hashrate. Each new block is the miner’s with probability \(\alpha\) and the others’ with probability \(1 - \alpha\).

  2. The state \((a, h)\) is the pair of lengths of the miner’s private branch and of the public one.

  3. In each state, the miner chooses whether to publish, wait or give up and adopt the public chain.

  4. When two branches tie, a fraction \(\gamma\) of the other miners build on the miner’s block.

A strategy \(\pi\) is a rule for what to do in each state, like a dict policy[(a, h)] = "wait". Over all strategies, the miner’s best long-run share of the blocks in the chain is

\[\rho^*(\alpha, \gamma) = \max_\pi \lim \frac{R_\text{miner}}{R_\text{miner} + R_\text{others}}.\]

In words: run the best strategy forever, and count what fraction of the final chain’s blocks are the miner’s.

A ratio is hard to optimize directly. A Markov decision problem is easier: choose an action in each state of a random process to maximize the average reward per step. Standard algorithms solve it, for example value iteration, which repeatedly improves an estimate of each state’s worth. The trick turns the ratio into such a problem:

  1. Guess a share \(\rho\).

  2. Pay the miner \(r_\text{miner} - \rho (r_\text{miner} + r_\text{others})\) per block: its own blocks minus \(\rho\) times all blocks.

  3. A share above \(\rho\) is achievable exactly when some strategy makes this reward positive on average. Solve the problem to find out.

  4. Raise or lower the guess, and repeat. This bisection, a binary search on a real number, finds \(\rho^*\).

What it shows:

  • Mining honestly earns \(\alpha\). So honest mining is an equilibrium exactly when \(\rho^* = \alpha\).

  • With \(\gamma = 0\), ties going to honest blocks, that holds up to about \(\alpha = 0.33\). A miner with less than about a third of the hashrate cannot gain by deviating.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.mining_games.optimal_mining_revenue() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.mining_games.honest_mining_is_equilibrium(), by relative value iteration. Both branches are capped at max_lead blocks, which can only underestimate the best deviation; the full games of Kiayias et al., with several strategic miners, are not modeled.

References: A. Kiayias, E. Koutsoupias, M. Kyropoulou and Y. Tselekounis, Blockchain mining games, Proc. ACM EC 2016, 365–382 (2016), arXiv:1607.02420; A. Sapirshtein, Y. Sompolinsky and A. Zohar, Optimal selfish mining strategies in Bitcoin, Financial Cryptography 2016, LNCS 9603, 515–532 (2017).

2017 – MakerDAO’s Dai: a Collateralized Stablecoin and Liquidation#

Coin prices swing wildly, which makes them awkward for payments. A stablecoin aims at a fixed price, usually one US dollar. Dai, launched in December 2017, is backed by ether locked in contracts rather than dollars in a bank. The locked ether is collateral: something of value pledged to secure a loan, as at a pawnshop.

  1. A user locks ether in a vault (then called a collateralized debt position).

  2. The user draws newly minted (created) Dai as a loan. The vault records this as its debt.

  3. An oracle keeps reporting the price of ether.

  4. The vault must always satisfy the rule below at that price.

\[\text{collateral} \times \text{price} \ge 1.5 \times \text{debt}\]

In words: the collateral must be worth at least 150% of the debt. Lock 3 ether at $100 each, worth $300, and you can draw up to 200 Dai.

If a fall in the price breaks this inequality:

  1. Anyone may liquidate the vault.

  2. The liquidator repays its debt in Dai, which burns that Dai: it is destroyed and leaves circulation.

  3. The liquidator takes collateral worth the debt plus a 13% penalty.

The margin is what keeps every Dai backed:

  • At the moment of liquidation, the vault holds collateral worth 1.5 times its debt. That is more than the 1.13 times the liquidator takes.

  • The price can fall by a third before the collateral is worth less than the debt, since \(1.5 \times 2/3 = 1\).

  • Dai becomes undercollateralized (backed by less than its value) only if the price falls faster than liquidations can follow. That happened on 12 March 2020. The network was congested, so transactions were delayed, and some liquidators won collateral for almost nothing.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.lending.VaultEngine, Stablecoin and PriceFeed, with Single-Collateral Dai’s 150% ratio and 13% penalty. The liquidator buys the collateral at the oracle price instead of in an auction, and there is no stability fee, savings rate or governance.

References: The Maker Team, The Dai stablecoin system (December 2017).

2018 – Uniswap’s Constant-Product Market Maker and Impermanent Loss#

An order book needs market makers who post and cancel orders constantly. That is too expensive on chain, where every update is a paid transaction.

Uniswap, launched in November 2018, replaced the order book with a pool: a contract holding reserves of two tokens, \(x\) of one and \(y\) of the other. Like Hanson’s market maker, a formula sets the price. The rule is that trades keep the product \(xy\) constant.

  1. A trader pays in \(\Delta x\) of the first token.

  2. The pool keeps 0.3% as a fee, so only \(0.997\,\Delta x\) counts.

  3. The pool pays out the \(\Delta y\) of the second token that restores the product. In integers, as Uniswap V2 computes it: dy = y * 997 * dx // (1000 * x + 997 * dx).

\[(x + 0.997\,\Delta x)(y - \Delta y) = x y.\]

In words: after the trade, the reserves, counting only the input after the fee, multiply to the same product as before.

  • Every trade moves the price. The pool’s price is the ratio \(p = y/x\). The more of a token is bought, the scarcer and dearer it becomes.

  • No trade can empty the pool. \(\Delta y\) is always less than \(y\).

Anyone can deposit both tokens and earn the fees. Such a depositor is a liquidity provider. It bears impermanent loss:

  1. The provider deposits at a price \(p\).

  2. The market price elsewhere moves by a factor \(r\).

  3. Arbitrageurs, traders who profit from price differences between markets, trade with the pool until its price matches. They buy from it the token that rose.

  4. With the product fixed, \(x = \sqrt{xy/p}\) and \(y = \sqrt{xy\,p}\). So the deposit’s value \(px + y = 2\sqrt{xy\,p}\) grows by a factor \(\sqrt{r}\).

  5. Holding the two tokens instead would have grown by \((1 + r)/2\).

The ratio \(2\sqrt{r}/(1+r)\) is at most one, with equality only at \(r = 1\). If the price quadruples, \(r = 4\), the ratio is \(4/5\): the deposit is worth 20% less than holding. The loss is impermanent because it vanishes if the price returns.

Uniswap V2 (2020) added a price oracle that reads the price before the first trade of each block. A transaction moves the price only after that reading, so no single transaction can move the oracle.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.amm.amount_out() (V2’s getAmountOut), blockchainkit.economics.systems.amm.impermanent_loss() and the contract blockchainkit.economics.systems.amm.ConstantProductPool, whose previous_price() stores the price before a block’s first trade rather than V2’s time-weighted accumulator. Shares are not tokens, and no liquidity is locked at the first deposit.

References: H. Adams, Uniswap whitepaper (November 2018); H. Adams, N. Zinsmeister and D. Robinson, Uniswap v2 core (March 2020).

2019 – Flash Boys 2.0: Front-Running and Priority Gas Auctions#

On Ethereum, a transaction’s fee is set by its gas: a unit of computing work. Each transaction offers a price per unit of gas. Miners then ordered transactions by fee, highest first.

Daian et al. watched arbitrage bots on decentralized exchanges. When an opportunity appeared, only the first transaction to reach it could take it. So the bots competed in a priority gas auction:

  1. A bot sends a transaction with some fee.

  2. A rival sees it in the public mempool and replaces its own pending transaction with one paying a higher fee. Each replacement must raise the fee by at least a fraction \(\beta\).

  3. The bots keep outbidding each other while a raise still leaves a profit.

  4. The miner puts the highest bid first, and that bot takes the opportunity.

Suppose every bot values the opportunity at \(V\). Bidding stops at a price whose next raise would exceed \(V\): \((1 + \beta)\,\text{price} > V\). The winner therefore keeps

\[V - \text{price} < V - \frac{V}{1 + \beta} = \frac{\beta}{1 + \beta}\, V,\]

and the rest goes in fees to whoever orders the block. In words: the winner keeps only a small slice of the prize. With \(\beta = 0.1\), it keeps less than \(1/11\), about 9%.

They called this value miner extractable value (MEV): what a block producer can capture by choosing which transactions go in a block and in what order. MEV also threatens consensus itself:

  • Suppose a past block holds more MEV than the reward for a new one.

  • Then a miner gains by forking the chain to re-mine that block and capture the MEV.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.ordering.priority_gas_auction(), in which bots respond one at a time with the minimum raise; real bots raced on latency, mempool visibility and gas estimates.

References: P. Daian, S. Goldfeder, T. Kell, Y. Li, X. Zhao, I. Bentov, L. Breidenbach and A. Juels, Flash Boys 2.0: frontrunning, transaction reordering, and consensus instability in decentralized exchanges, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2020 (2019), arXiv:1904.05234.

2020 – Flash Loans and Oracle Manipulation: the bZx Attacks#

In February 2020, two attacks hit the bZx lending protocol. Both used flash loans to move a decentralized exchange’s price within one transaction. Then they exploited protocols that trusted that price.

A flash loan must be repaid in the same transaction. If it is not, the whole transaction reverts: it is undone, like an exception that rolls back every change. The lender can never lose its money, so it needs no collateral. Anyone can briefly command a large sum.

In its simplest form, the attack uses a constant-product pool holding \(x\) tokens and \(y\) dollars. The spot price is the pool’s price right now, \(y/x\).

  1. The attacker flash-borrows \(U\) dollars.

  2. It spends them on the token in the pool. The pool is left with \(y + U\) dollars and \(xy/(y+U)\) tokens. So the attacker receives \(xU/(y+U)\) tokens, and the spot price rises to \((y+U)^2/(xy)\).

  3. At that price, the tokens bought appear worth \(U(y + U)/y\), more than the \(U\) they cost.

  4. The attacker deposits them with a lender that reads the spot price and demands 150% collateral, and borrows against them.

  5. It repays the flash loan from the new loan and keeps the difference.

The loan in step 4 is

\[\frac{U(y + U)}{1.5\,y} > U \quad\text{whenever}\quad U > \frac{y}{2}.\]

In words: once the attacker spends more than half the pool’s dollars, the new loan exceeds the flash loan, and the attack profits. With \(y = 1{,}000{,}000\) and \(U = 2{,}000{,}000\), the attacker borrows 4,000,000 and keeps 2,000,000.

  • The lender loses. It is left holding tokens worth far less than it lent once the price recovers.

  • The defence is an older price. A price recorded before the transaction, like Uniswap V2’s, cannot be moved this way.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.lending.OracleLender, reading ConstantProductPool’s spot price or its price before the block, attacked by OracleAttacker with FlashLender. The model reduces the second attack to its mechanism; the first combined a margin trade on bZx with the manipulated price, across several protocols.

References: K. Qin, L. Zhou, B. Livshits and A. Gervais, Attacking the DeFi ecosystem with flash loans for fun and profit, Financial Cryptography 2021, LNCS 12674, 3–32 (2021). DOI.

2020 – EIP-1559’s Base Fee and Burn, with Roughgarden’s Analysis#

Bitcoin sells block space by a first-price auction: each user bids a fee, and the block’s producer takes the highest bids. Ethereum did the same until the London upgrade of August 2021. Vickrey’s analysis predicts the problems:

  • users must guess how much to shade their bids;

  • no bid is obviously right;

  • prices swing with demand.

EIP-1559 instead posts a price, like a price tag in a shop, and adjusts it with demand. That price is the base fee \(b_t\) per unit of gas.

  1. Each block has a gas target \(g^*\), half the block’s capacity.

  2. Every transaction pays the base fee, which is burned: destroyed, so that nobody receives it.

  3. Every transaction also pays a tip to the block’s producer.

  4. After each block, the base fee moves by up to an eighth. It goes up when the block used more gas \(g_t\) than the target, and down when it used less.

\[b_{t+1} = b_t \left(1 + \frac{1}{8}\, \frac{g_t - g^*}{g^*}\right).\]

In words: a full block raises the base fee by 12.5%, an empty block lowers it by 12.5%, and a block exactly at the target leaves it unchanged. It works like a thermostat.

Roughgarden’s analysis showed why this helps:

  • Bidding is easy. The price is posted rather than bid. So, outside sudden rises in demand, bidding the base fee plus a small tip is optimal for users.

  • Producers cannot inflate it. The base fee is burned. So a producer gains nothing by raising it with fake transactions.

  • Producers have no reason to deviate. A myopic producer, one that cares only about the current block, does best by including the highest tips.

  • Side deals do not pay. Users and producers cannot profit from an off-chain deal that skips the base fee, since it goes to neither of them.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.fee_market.next_base_fee(), the specification’s integer update, and blockchainkit.economics.systems.fee_market.simulate_fee_market(), which runs a mempool through either mechanism. Every transaction is a transfer of 21,000 gas, values are uniform, and first-price bidders shade by a fixed fraction rather than strategically.

References: V. Buterin, E. Conner, R. Dudley, M. Slipper, I. Norden and A. Bakhta, EIP-1559: fee market change for ETH 1.0 chain (2019); T. Roughgarden, Transaction fee mechanism design for the Ethereum blockchain: an economic analysis of EIP-1559 (December 2020), arXiv:2012.00854.

2021 – Sandwich Attacks on Decentralized Exchanges#

A swap is a trade with a pool. Before it is mined, it waits in the public mempool, where anyone can see it. The victim’s only protection is its slippage tolerance \(s\): its swap reverts if it would receive less than a fraction \(1 - s\) of the output quoted before the attack.

A sandwich attacker wraps the victim’s swap between two of its own:

  1. The attacker sees the victim’s purchase in the mempool.

  2. It front-runs: it buys the same token, an amount \(a\), just before the victim. That raises the price the victim pays.

  3. The victim’s swap runs at the higher price and pushes the price up further.

  4. The attacker back-runs: it sells just after, at that higher price.

A larger front-run \(a\) moves the price more and earns the attacker more. But the victim’s output \(\text{out}_\text{victim}(a)\) falls as \(a\) grows, and the attacker gets nothing if the victim’s swap reverts. So the attacker maximizes its profit over the front-runs that keep

\[\text{out}_\text{victim}(a) \ge (1 - s)\,\text{out}_\text{victim}(0),\]

that is, after the front-run, the victim must still get at least \(1 - s\) times what it would have got with no attack.

  • Large trades suffer most. For a large victim trade, the best attack is the largest front-run allowed.

  • Small trades are safe. The attacker pays the pool’s fee twice. So a trade too small to move the price by more than those two fees is not worth attacking.

Zhou, Qin, Torres, Le and Gervais derived this optimal attack and measured sandwiches on Uniswap. They showed that a looser tolerance hands more of each trade to the attacker. Responses include:

  • private transaction relays, which send a transaction straight to a block producer and skip the public mempool;

  • batch auctions, which execute a batch of orders together at one price, so their order no longer matters.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.ordering.sandwich_profit() and blockchainkit.economics.systems.ordering.sandwich_attack(), which bounds the front-run by bisection and maximizes the profit by ternary search; the attacker is assumed to control the order of the three transactions and to hold the capital.

References: L. Zhou, K. Qin, C. F. Torres, D. V. Le and A. Gervais, High-frequency trading on decentralized on-chain exchanges, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2021 (2021), arXiv:2009.14021.

2022 – Proposer-Builder Separation and MEV-Boost#

Extracting MEV takes searchers (bots that find MEV opportunities), order flow (a stream of users’ transactions) and infrastructure. A validator that has them earns more per unit of stake than one that does not. So stakers, people who entrust their coins to a validator, move to it. MEV pushes proof of stake toward a few large operators.

Proposer-builder separation splits the job in two. Specialized builders assemble blocks and bid for each slot, the 12-second window in which one block is added. The proposer, any validator, only signs the best bid.

  1. The protocol picks a validator as proposer for the slot.

  2. Each builder \(b\) assembles a block. The block holds fees \(f\) and an MEV opportunity \(M\), of which the builder can capture a fraction \(\sigma_b\). So it values the block at \(v_b = f + \sigma_b M\).

  3. In a second-price auction, the builders bid these values.

  4. The proposer signs the winning block. It receives the runner-up’s value, or what it could build itself if that is more.

\[\text{revenue} \ge v_{(2)} = f + \sigma_{(2)} M.\]

In words: the proposer earns at least the second-highest builder’s value for the block.

  • Skill no longer matters. This bound does not depend on the proposer’s own skill.

  • Small stakers keep up. With two or more capable builders, \(\sigma_{(2)}\) is close to one. So a solo staker earns about as much per slot as a professional operator.

  • Trust remains. Flashbots’ MEV-Boost ran the auction outside the protocol from Ethereum’s move to proof of stake in September 2022. It runs through relays, middlemen that hold the builder’s block until the proposer signs. Both builders and proposers must trust them. Enshrining the auction in the protocol remains open.

Implementation: blockchainkit.economics.systems.pbs.simulate_pbs(), which picks proposers with StakeSampler and sells each slot by second_price_auction(). MEV-Boost’s auction is first-price with open bids through relays; with many builders the price approaches the runner-up’s value either way.

References: V. Buterin, Proposer/block builder separation-friendly fee market designs, ethresear.ch (June 2021); Flashbots, MEV-Boost (2022).
